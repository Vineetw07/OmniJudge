Alright — playing this exactly as I would with 20 wins behind me and a submission clock running. Forget "best product." Judges reviewing thousands of entries over a 10-day window aren't falling in love with anything — they're pattern-matching for reasons to trust a submission or reasons to distrust it, fast. Here's the order I'd spend hours in, ranked by what actually moves a judge's hand, not by what feels impressive to build.

1. A demo that cannot break — this is non-negotiable, above everything else.
Every veteran has watched a genuinely strong project score low because docker compose up failed on the judge's machine, or a role check flaked under a second run. One crash and a judge stops trusting everything else in the repo, including the parts that were actually good. Before you write one more feature: pull your repo fresh into an empty folder, kill the network, run docker compose up cold, twice in a row, and watch it seed and serve without you touching anything. This is worth more than any single feature you could add in the same hours.

2. The exact 7 checks in run.py — run them until you're bored of running them.
Not "I'm pretty sure this works." Run it after every meaningful change. The peer-scores check in particular — run it, watch it fail once on purpose (comment out your guard), confirm you understand why it failed, then fix it and watch it pass. That muscle memory is what stops it silently regressing at hour 60 when you're tired and refactoring something unrelated.

3. Role isolation — because it's the rare spot with triple leverage.
Most rubric items only feed one score. This one feeds three: it's an auto-DQ trigger, it's 25% of your weighted score (Judging Integrity), and it's the entire deciding factor for Best Judging Engine. Nothing else in this spec pays out on three axes at once. Spend disproportionate time here relative to how small the code actually is.

4. JUDGING.md, written like you're defending a thesis, not filling out a template.
Here's the thing veterans know that first-timers don't: for the 25% integrity weight, a judge can't inspect your database mid-review — your writing is the only evidence they see directly, alongside the checker's pass/fail. Make it read like a real technical write-up: state the method, show the math, and narrate the zero-variance judge trap as a story — "we hit this, here's why naive normalization breaks, here's the fix." A judge who reads that stops treating you like "one of 5000" and starts treating you like someone who actually understands the problem. That shift in perception is worth more than a UI polish pass.

5. A README a skeptical, tired judge can verify in 90 seconds.
Claimed tiers, verified tiers, one honest limitations list, one command to run. This is the very first thing every judge opens. If it's muddled or oversold, you've lost trust before they've read a line of code — and trust, once lost, doesn't come back over the rest of the review.

6. Only now — visual polish, hard-timeboxed.
Everyone in this hackathon has an AI IDE. A clean UI is table stakes, not a differentiator, which is exactly why it's only 15% of score. Spend an hour making the gallery and dashboard look intentional. Do not spend three.

7. Reserve the final stretch purely for freeze rehearsal — no new code.
The single most avoidable way to lose a hackathon is shipping the deadline instead of shipping before it. Veterans build in a buffer: last few hours are fresh-clone-and-run only, re-record the video if anything changed, confirm the repo is actually public, confirm .dogfood.toml claims match the report you're committing. No feature is worth touching in that window.

If I had to compress this into one sentence: build less than you think you can, verify it more than feels necessary, and write about it like you're proud of it — that combination is rarer than a good feature set, and it's what actually separates a top-5 finish from "one of 5000 good-looking repos."
